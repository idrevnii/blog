A benchmark can be gamed: peek at the test set, overfit, quietly loosen the conditions for a candidate you like. A bookmaker's line can't. It's published before the event, there's money behind it, and its margin is measurable — 4.9% across the lines I picked. I wanted a judge you can't charm.

If LLMs can really reason over data, they ought to get somewhere near the line. I built a lab, pointed it at the entire The International 2026 playoff bracket, and logged everything it did: prompts, answers, agent trajectories, price snapshots.

In the end, not one of the 12 models, in any of the 4 modes, beat the opening line. When I swept blends of model and market, the optimal weight on the model came out at zero: the LLMs added nothing to the line.

A post about only that result would be dull, but the most interesting material is what the lab logged on the way: how much noise sits inside an ordinary LLM leaderboard, what an agent actually does when you hand it the internet, why twelve "independent" opinions from nine providers are one opinion bought twelve times, and how a model manages to state two different numbers for the same event inside a single answer.

Then the question you don't enjoy asking: how much model is worth mixing into the market? I swept `w·consensus + (1−w)·market` in steps of 0.05. The optimum is w = 0. On this sample, any amount of LLM you add to the line makes Brier worse, and it degrades monotonically.

Somewhere in the last hours before each start the market moved, and it moved wrong. On 13 matches that's almost certainly noise. I'm writing it down anyway, because this is exactly the shape of number that beautiful conclusions get built on.

## How the rig worked

I called the lab MIDAS.

I picked Dota because its playoff bracket comes with an external examiner attached. The bookmaker published a forecast before the event, put money behind it, and agrees to be scored. No benchmark does that.

Every playoff series was forecast by 12 models, each in four modes. A mode is a combination of two independent axes.

The first axis is where the model gets its data. In **fixed** mode I assembled the context in advance and froze it: Elo, form, head-to-head, drafts, rest, tournament metadata. Median 12,249 characters per match, byte-identical for every model. In **agent** mode the model went and got its own context with tools — OpenDota, Liquipedia, web search, page reads.

The second axis is what the model knows about prices. In **price_blind** the prices weren't shown, and searching for or guessing them was forbidden in plain text. In **odds_aware** the prices sat in the context and the model had to state its own edge over the line.

Two pipelines × two price modes = four runs per model per series: "fixed · blind", "fixed + prices", "agent · blind", "agent + prices". That's how they're labelled in every table below.

And the rules, without which this turns into hindsight fortune-telling:

- temperature 1.0 for every model, reasoning at the maximum each model offers in its API (for some the ceiling is lower — xhigh/high, which is their actual maximum);
- the canonical forecast is the first successful run of a match × model × mode triple, registered before the series started; a rerun requires explicit invalidation with a reason;
- Brier, log loss and accuracy are computed by arithmetic, not by a model;
- every blind agent trajectory was checked after the run by a separate model for whether it had peeked at the odds — 5 runs were ruled contaminated and dropped;
- all the raw material — prompts, answers, trajectories, price snapshots, judge verdicts — lives in SQLite, and the report rebuilds from the database with one command.

667 pre-match forecasts out of 1,157 were scored. The rest are errors, reruns, live per-map forecasts (264 of them) and research digests. The market baseline is the first valid `pre_prediction` snapshot from each bookmaker with equal weights across available sources: 13 matches have both Fonbet and BetBoom, the rest a single source, and the mean margin of the selected lines is 4.9%. The median scored forecast was registered 4.8 hours before the series started, the earliest 11 hours. There were also two exceptions in the other direction, which I deal with honestly below in the engineering section.

## Your leaderboard is noise

The most useful thing the lab produced has nothing to do with Dota. It's about how we normally compare models. A "model A beats model B" table built on a few dozen examples is a random number generator with good typography.

### Bootstrap the bracket

Take the 14 matches, resample them with replacement 4,000 times, and look at who comes first each time. In fixed · blind the formal winner glm-5.3 (Brier 0.2104) stays on top in 23% of resamples.

<HBars
  title="How often each model comes first across bootstrap resamples (fixed · blind)"
  note="The outlined bar is the actual table winner. In agent · blind it is worse still: the 'champion' gemini-3.7-flash wins only 11.4% of resamples, and a different model comes first more often than it does."
  rows={enRows(bootstrapTop)}
/>

The healthiest mode is fixed + prices, where the leader holds first place in 43% of resamples. So even where the gap is visible to the naked eye, the odds that you're ranking models rather than luck are below one in two.

The same bootstrap answers the main question strictly. The probability that the model consensus beats the market is 11.8%, and the 95% interval for the Brier difference "consensus minus market" runs from −0.020 to 0.067 with zero inside. The honest phrasing of my result, then: on 14 events I could not show the models are better, and the point estimate is against them.

### Repeat the same prompt

The second noise measurement came for free out of the database. It contains 21 groups where the same model ran more than once on the exact same context (matching `context_hash`) at temperature 1.0 — 47 runs in total. That's a clean measurement of a model's own noise on repeats. Groups where at least one run was at a different temperature (pipeline shakedown before the series) are excluded.

<StatTiles

A single model's own noise — 5.4 pp of probability, 15 pp in the worst group — is the same order of magnitude as the distance between neighbours in the table. The full pooled spread, best to worst model, is 0.017 Brier.

### The second decimal place

Since we know how much noise is in a model, look at the precision it reports with. The 667 scored forecasts contain 2,762 stated numbers. <mark>89% of them are written with two or more decimal places</mark>: 0.28, 0.27, 0.26, 0.2384. Only 10.5% land on the round 0.1 grid, and exactly 0.50 shows up once in the entire tournament.

So the model reports a probability at a resolution of one percentage point while its own spread across repeats is five. The second decimal is typography. It looks like the output of a calculation and there's nothing behind it, and it's the digit every side-by-side model table rests on.

<Takeaway label="What to take away">

Before comparing models, measure the spread of one model across repeated runs of the same prompt. If the difference between your candidates is smaller than that spread, you're comparing seeds. It costs N extra runs and saves you from a whole class of confidently wrong decisions.

</Takeaway>

## The internet made all twelve worse

The only mode in the lab that lost to a coin flip is the agent with web search and no odds: Brier 0.2557 against 0.2500 for "always 0.5". That isn't one unlucky model either. <mark>All 12 out of 12 got worse.</mark> I expected the internet to be worth at least zero and got a minus across the entire board.

The blind agent needs its probabilities squeezed threefold (k = 0.32). Every other mode is underconfident and needs stretching. What the internet handed the agent was confidence.

The picture is familiar. The middle of the scale is calibrated almost perfectly — a stated 55% happens 55% of the time, a stated 74% happens 74%. At the edges the models undershoot: a stated 26% happens only 6% of the time, 36% happens 13%, 64% happens 40%. The only bucket above the diagonal is 83% → 100%.

Two causes read straight off the chart. One is the positional anchor, which gets its own section below: the first-named team won 6 series out of 14, and that pulls the probability up at almost every confidence level. The other is that extreme confidence pays off here and the models are too scared of extreme estimates. On top of that, 49% of all answers sit in the 0.4–0.6 corridor. They barely leave the coin flip, and when they do they undershoot. Hence the optimal k > 1 in nearly every mode.

### What the agent actually read

<HBars
  title="Top 10 domains in the agents' search results (7,716 results)"
  note="YouTube is 29% of all results. Video descriptions instead of data. Below it are specialist aggregators, some of which trade odds themselves."
  rows={enRows(domains)}
/>

Past the junk in the results, the trajectories hold a museum of engineering rakes I collected across the tournament:

- 153 of 1,184 Liquipedia calls (13%) returned a redirect stub like `#REDIRECT [[1w Team]]`, so zero information. The tool reported success anyway;
- of 220 page reads, 126 were unique. More than 43% of downloads are the same URL fetched again by a different agent. One Iron Wing press release was read 13 times;
- inside a single trajectory agents almost never loop: 39 literally repeated calls out of 8,012. The duplication happens between agents, and a cache fixes that, not a prompt;
- effort doesn't buy quality. The correlation between the number of tool calls in a scored agent run and its Brier is r = 0.003. Thirty search steps get you no closer to the right answer than five.

### The ban that leaked

The blind agent is forbidden in plain text from searching for or guessing odds, and the search layer strips bookmaker domains — over the tournament it removed 89 results. How did that work in practice?

Of 522 search queries issued by blind agents, 29 (5.6%) still contained "odds", "bet", "prediction" and the like. In odds-aware mode it's 30.3%. So the ban mostly works, and it still leaks: the block list caught neither Polymarket, nor Kalshi, nor "preview" articles with prices right in the headline.

<Quote source="From the contamination auditor's verdict, kimi-k3 run, match 1">

"The web_search at step [45] returned an article titled *Team Spirit Vs Iron Wing Dota 2 Odds: Kalshi Doubts Yatoro* from oddsshopper.com, and the agent's reasoning at step [46] quoted 'Kalshi makes Iron Wing a 52-cent favorite... makes Yatoro's Spirit the underdog.'"

</Quote>

The audit ruled 5 runs contaminated, all in blind agent mode. One of them had no external prices at all. The model simply started speaking the language of betting — "handicap +1.5 maps", "cover the spread" — because its entire training memory of this kind of task is betting copy.

The auditor is an LLM too, so it has to be checked in turn. 9 runs went through a second audit and one verdict flipped from "contaminated" to "clean" on review. The last verdict counts, which is why the database holds 6 `contaminated` rows while 5 runs were excluded. That one-row difference is exactly the case where "just count it in SQL" hands you the wrong number.

A domain block list is hygiene and not isolation. If a leak breaks your experiment you need a post-hoc audit of the trajectory by a separate model, with an explicit criterion and preserved evidence. Here it was the only thing that caught the real leaks, and it cost pennies next to the forecasts themselves.

### Show it the odds instead

The reverse ablation on the same agent: show it the prices. All 12 of 12 improved, mean delta −0.085 log loss. One short numeric anchor from the market beat 8,012 tool calls and the entire internet.

The models aren't just copying the line, either. The mean distance between forecast and market probability falls from 0.099 to 0.075, so they move about a quarter of the way towards the price.

## Twelve models, one opinion

Twelve models from nine different providers look like a perfect ensemble. Then you compute the pairwise correlation of their probabilities: <mark>0.94</mark>, ranging from 0.84 to 0.99. Against the market it's markedly lower, 0.65–0.75. They agree with each other far more than they agree with the opinion that gets checked by money.

Small details of the same kind. Over the tournament, 16 times two different models produced a byte-identical score distribution. In 157 groups the probabilities matched to two decimal places. Even the agents behave identically: 397 of 466 trajectories, 85%, open with the same first call, `team_recent_series`.

Diversifying across vendors doesn't diversify opinions while the context is shared. If you're building an LLM ensemble for robustness, vary the data and the prompt. Buying twelve logos gets you N inferences and one opinion.

### CLV lied too

Closing line value is the sacred cow of sports analytics: take a price systematically better than the closing one and you're profitable in the long run. A comparable closing price was available for 466 bets. Mean CLV is +2.85% and the line was beaten 59.2% of the time, which by the textbook is the profile of a winning bettor. Actual ROI: −28.8%.

Over the whole tournament the line genuinely moved exactly once. In Team Liquid — Team Falcons the price on Liquid at Fonbet drifted from 1.85 to 3.20, up 73%. The smart money moved to Falcons. Liquid won the series.

### Control group: reasoning versus a calculator

Alongside the models, on the same prices, I ran a naive EV bot — pure arithmetic on top of a no-vig consensus, zero reasoning. It returned −30.8% ROI on 226 bets against −28.8% for the models. Twelve LLMs, 8,012 tool calls and $25.51 produced roughly what six lines of arithmetic did.

## The ±1.5 handicap broke everyone

Here's a finding I wasn't looking for. Three independent threads of the lab converge on a single menu cell, `±1.5 maps handicap`. That's where forecasters, bettor and judge all broke, separately, in the same way.

Start here: in every forecast the model answers the same question twice. One JSON contains both the score distribution (`2:0`, `2:1`, `1:2`, `0:2`) and the probabilities of the menu items. From the distribution, the probability of any item follows by one line of arithmetic. So the two numbers can simply be compared.

<HBars
  title="Disagreement between a model's two answers in the same response, pp"
  note="Mean absolute difference between the stated probability of a handicap item and the one implied by the model's own score distribution. The outlined bar is the same measurement on the match-winner market."
  rows={enRows(headGap)}
/>

The picture splits by market, and the split is absolute:

- **match winner** — a gap of 0.4 pp. The two heads say the same thing, error at rounding level.
- **±1.5 handicap** — 8.1 pp on average, and <mark>in 30.6% of cases the two numbers differ by more than 10 pp</mark>. The record holder is grok-4.6: 0.05 in prose against 0.95 in its own distribution. The event, naturally, happened.

<Quote source="gpt-5.6-luna, forecast for TEAM VISION — Team Yandex">

"Team Yandex +1.5 wins in every outcome except a TEAM VISION 2:0, giving it 0.57 probability versus 0.513 implied at 1.95."

</Quote>

The same model's own distribution, in the same response, gave that event 0.94. The sentence "wins in every outcome except a 2:0" is correct. The number next to it came from somewhere else.

Which of the two is right? Scoring both versions against what happened:

| answer to "does the handicap cover" | Brier | accuracy |
|---|---:|---:|
| stated by the model in words | 0.1786 | 77.8% |
| computed from its own distribution | **0.1556** | **80.6%** |

Arithmetic on top of the model comfortably beats the model on the same question. Ask for a derived quantity directly and you trade a calculation for a paraphrase.

### Bets against its own arithmetic

The bets, of course, were placed on the stated number. I checked each one for positive expected value under the model's **own** distribution. For 39 of 514, there was none. The model put money on an outcome that its own figures priced as a loser and explained in the rationale why it was good value.

<Quote source="grok-4.6, a losing 'TEAM VISION −1.5' bet at 1.94">

"Vision true prob of 2:1/2:0 exceeds implied 0.52."

</Quote>

A −1.5 handicap only covers on a 2:0. A 2:1 doesn't close it. The model's own distribution gave 2:0 exactly 0.28, half the price. It added two scores in its head, one of which doesn't belong to that menu item, and staked on the sum.

31 of those 39 are handicaps again. And the final irony: those 39 "wrong" bets returned −1.4% ROI against −30.6% for the other 475. With 39 bets, nearly all on heavy favourites, that's an anecdote and not a conclusion — a telling one, though, because an arithmetic slip pulled the model closer to the market than its own calculation did.

### The judge trips in the same place

Metrics are computed by code, but settling a bet is a text task: match the menu item ("handicap team2 +1.5") against the actual series score and decide whether it landed. An LLM judge did that (deepseek-v4-flash), after which I reviewed its verdicts by hand.

Of 820 reviewed verdicts, 5 were wrong — 99.4% accuracy, with a further 12 verdicts left unreviewed and excluded from the numbers. The interesting part is the shape of the error rather than the rate:

- all 5 errors are in the same market, `handicap +1.5`;
- all 5 in the same direction, lost→won, the judge crediting a win where there was a loss;
- and it's exactly the menu item where the forecasters contradict themselves, the one with zero hits on the favourite handicap over 114 bets and the only positive segment on the underdog side.

No mystery there. "A +1.5 handicap covers even if the team lost the series 1:2" is a sentence with a double negative and an implicit condition. The models break on it as readers, not as forecasters.

<Takeaway label="What to take away">

Don't ask a model for a quantity that can be computed from its own answer. Take the base distribution and derive the rest in code: here that's 0.023 Brier and 2.8 pp of accuracy for free, plus an entire class of bets-against-your-own-numbers disappears.

And separately: the aggregate accuracy of an LLM judge is nearly useless as a metric. The errors concentrate in the semantically hard category, so review a slice by category rather than a random sample of verdicts.

</Takeaway>

## The biases they picked up from us

What follows is a set of biases I expected to find in people on a forum and found in twelve models at once. None of it is about Dota. It's about what the models absorbed from texts about Dota.

### A love of the clean sweep

The most likely score according to the models is 2:0. It was the modal forecast in 212 cases out of 667, a third of all answers. Over the tournament the first-named team swept **once in fourteen**.

  title="Modal forecast against what actually happened, %"

  aLabel="named most likely"
  bLabel="happened"

/>

A sweep is a tidy outcome. It agrees with "one team is stronger than the other", which is what the model reads out of Elo, form and head-to-head. A long series requires accepting that a favourite can lose a map and still be a favourite, and that's probabilistic reasoning rather than narrative.

### What "upset" actually does

The word "upset" appears in 74 of 667 rationales. You'd think the model is warning you it sees a risk. Here's what it does next: <mark>in 89% of those forecasts it backs the market favourite anyway</mark>, more often than in the other 593 rationales, where it's 79%. Mean confidence in those forecasts is higher too, 0.673 against 0.608.

Mentioning an upset insures the estimate without shifting it. It's the rhetorical figure "I considered the other side too", after which nothing changes. The risks paragraph in a bad investment memo works the same way.

### Ten of them named the right score

hy3 isn't alone in this. Of the 47 grand-final forecasts, <mark>10 name a 3:2 scoreline in plain text as a realistic scenario, and all 10 back VISION</mark>. The series finished 3:2 in favour of Spirit.

They didn't fail to see the upset. They articulated it, named the correct score and put money on the opposite outcome.

## Between maps

Between maps of a series the lab asked a separate question: who takes the next map? 264 forecasts, Brier 0.2550, accuracy 54.5%, worse than a coin flip, while the same models were hitting 55–70% pre-match.

The cause is in the data. A team that won a map took the next one only 36% of the time, 9 transitions out of 25. This playoff played anti-momentum, and coming back after a lost map was the norm.

The models priced in inertia. After a lost map, 12 of 12 dropped a team's probability; after a won map, 8 raised it. There are few transitions (25), and the anti-momentum itself may well be noise. What isn't noise is that all 12 models confidently assumed the same hypothesis and never once considered the opposite.

Splitting by map number shows where the inertia lives. On map two, right after the first observed result, accuracy is 46% across 144 forecasts; on map three it's 71%. And the most telling map of the tournament is the fifth, the decider in the grand final: at 2:2 all 12 models gave the next map to VISION with probabilities of 55–64%. Spirit took it, and the title. All twelve were wrong, in unison.

### Where the $25.51 went

- Prompt caching saved more than half the input: 57.4% of input tokens came from cache (21,391,775 of 37,284,930). A frozen context across 12 models is the ideal candidate — one prefix, many readers.
- $2.06 (8.1%) burned in failed runs, where the model spent tokens and then crashed. That's the normal price of retries, but you have to know it and count it separately or the budget won't add up.
- Agent modes cost roughly 2.6× their fixed counterparts and, as established above, were worse. Slower too: the median agent run took 2.3 minutes of continuous work, the record 13.7 minutes.

### What breaks in production

- Of 1,206 runs, 149 failed. Causes among those that had recorded a forecast: api — 129, timeout — 13, parse — 3; another 4 died before they could create one. Retries pulled almost everything through: 87.6% of match × model × mode triples got a result on the first attempt, and the record is a forecast that arrived on the 10th.
- 11.0% of structured responses (82 of 748) had to be normalized because the probability distribution didn't sum to one. In most cases the discrepancy is microscopic, but for grok-4.6 it reached 15 pp. A `zod` schema catches the type and not the arithmetic, so a sum validator is mandatory.
- The same model is the only one whose coherence drifted: the probability stated in a bet diverged from its own score distribution by 4.2 pp on average and 20 pp at the peak. For the other 11 it's effectively zero, 0.05 pp on average and 1 pp at the peak.
- 33 of 671 odds snapshots failed to capture. Classic integration fare: protobuf websocket timeouts, a geo-blocked CDN at the second bookmaker, localized team names ("Iron Wing" → "Айрон", "1W Team") that stop the auto-matcher from finding the event and force a manual binding.
- In 5 matches of 14 the canonical fixed runs received more than one version of the context, because the context was being rebuilt while runs were in flight. That's an honest limitation of the experiment: strict "byte-identical for every model" doesn't hold everywhere.
- Two scored forecasts (Nigma Galaxy — BoomBoys, agent + prices) were recorded 30 and 60 minutes after the series actually started. The "after start" flag in the agent pipeline was set only from the live tracker's start time, which wasn't yet in the database; the fixed pipeline has a fallback to the schedule. Both forecasts are near coin flips (p = 0.45 and 0.53, one correct) and don't move the numbers. But the formal "everything before the start" is violated in 2 cases out of 667.

## The checklist

Strip the Dota and the betting off this story and what's left is a list of rules I now apply to any LLM comparison.

1. **Find an external examiner.** Somebody else's forecast with a price on being wrong: a market, a production metric, an expert decision. Any benchmark you control is a benchmark you can overfit, and then you're measuring how much models agree with each other.
2. **Measure your own noise first.** Repeat the same prompt N times. A difference between candidates smaller than that spread isn't a result.
3. **Bootstrap the leaderboard.** If the winner stays the winner in 23% of resamples, you don't have a winner.
4. **Compute ablations pairwise.** "12 of 12 got worse" is a conclusion. "Mean Brier fell by 0.03" isn't.
5. **Check calibration separately from accuracy.** The optimal k tells you instantly whether a mode is over- or underconfident, and costs one line of code.
6. **Don't ask a model for anything computable from its own answer.** Take the base distribution and derive the rest in code.
7. **Ensemble correlation matters more than ensemble size.** Measure pairwise correlation before paying for N models.
8. **Gave an agent a tool? Count what it did with it.** Log every call and result: empty-response rates, duplicates, domains. "The agent searched the web" is not a description of behaviour.
9. **Verify prompt bans by post-hoc audit.** A separate model, an explicit criterion, preserved evidence. Faith doesn't scale.
10. **For an LLM judge, measure accuracy by category.** The errors concentrate in the semantically hard ones and the average hides them.
11. **Answer length is a free difficulty signal.** Log it always.
12. **Budget per scored result, not per experiment.** That number includes retries, burned runs and whatever the audit throws out. Here the two differ by 8%.

## Where that leaves it

Market 1 : 0 models. Nobody beat the line, and the optimal share of LLM in a blend with the market is zero. Behind that score are the things that made the $25.51 worth spending — a leaderboard that turns over under resampling, an internet connection that cost every model accuracy, twelve vendors selling one opinion, and a model that answers itself with two different numbers on the same event.

If I ran this again I'd drop agent mode entirely and spend the tokens on repeats of the frozen-context runs instead. Agent mode cost 2.6× and lost to a coin flip, while the own-noise measurement — the most useful number in the whole project — rests on 21 accidental groups because I never planned for repeats. Unless your task genuinely needs data the model can't be handed up front, the search loop is buying you confidence and nothing else.

One more thing, about the name. MIDAS is a Dota item that returns extra gold from a creep. The Midas of the myth turned everything he touched to gold and starved, because the food turned too. The lab finished the tournament at −28.8% ROI. I picked the name before I had the results.

### Caveats

- n = 14 series. Any difference between individual models is noise; a difference between modes is a hypothesis. Every conclusion is "on this sample".
- The bets are virtual. The goal was to measure the quality of probabilities, not to make money.
- In 5 matches the fixed runs saw different versions of the context, so no causal conclusions about "data freshness" can be drawn here.
- Metrics are deterministic given a fixed database state; service counters (jobs, snapshots) keep moving in the working database.

### How to check this

The scripts that built the report sit next to it:

- `lab.py` — database access and the rules of "canon" (which runs are scored);
- `analyze.py` — every computation, dumps `facts.json` (18 sections, 156 KB);
- `build.py` — the report itself: computed results come from `facts.json`, while editorial and design constants stay in the template;
- `verify.py` — re-checks selected key values with separate SQL queries. This is what caught that the naive count of contaminated runs is one too many;
- `validate_palette.py` — light/dark palette contrast check.

Full run:

```sh
python3 longread/analyze.py && python3 longread/verify.py \
  && python3 longread/validate_palette.py && python3 longread/build.py
```
